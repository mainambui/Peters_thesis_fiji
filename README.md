Statistical Analysis
#Maina Mbui
All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3)
We employed a comprehensive, reproducible pipeline integrating data validation, univariate and multivariate modelling, and robust diagnostic checks to assess the effects of environmental and spatial predictors on fish assemblage structure and function.
Response variable categorization and rationale - To address complementary ecological and management questions, we organized our response variables into three distinct analytical configurations. This approach allows for a nuanced interpretation of results, moving beyond a single model for all responses and instead grouping metrics by their theoretical and applied relevance (Mouillot et al., 2013; Villéger et al., 2008).
1)	Core ecosystem structure & function: This configuration combined biomass, abundance, Functional Richness (FRic), and Functional Evenness (FEve). Biomass and abundance are fundamental measures of community structure and energy flow. FRic represents the volume of functional space occupied, indicating the diversity of ecological strategies, while FEve quantifies the regularity of species abundance distribution in that space, linked to resource-use efficiency. This integrated set provides a holistic view of ecosystem state, relevant for holistic, ecosystem-based management.
2)	Functional diversity & trait-based assembly: This configuration focused specifically on trait-based indices: FRic, FEve, Functional Divergence (FDiv), Functional Dispersion (FDis), and Rao's Quadratic Entropy (RaoQ). FDiv measures how abundance is distributed towards the extremities of the functional space, FDis is the mean distance of species to the centroid of functional space, and RaoQ integrates species’ pairwise functional differences and their abundances. This suite of metrics is designed to detect how environmental predictors (or filters) control species traits and functional roles (Mason et al., 2005), making it critical for assessing community resilience to climate change and for conservation prioritisation.
3)	 Fisheries relevant production: This configuration targeted biomass and abundance. As direct proxies for fish production and standing stock, these metrics are of paramount importance for fisheries management and evaluating the performance of community conservation areas in achieving biodiversity conservation objectives.
Data preparation and validation - We implemented a reproducible data cleaning and validation pipeline. All character variables were converted to factors. Rows with missing values in key response variables, biomass, abundance, and functional diversity indices (FRic, FEve, FDiv, FDis, RaoQ), were removed. To ensure statistical reliability, we constructed unique site identifiers and excluded any location with fewer than three transects. An automated validation routine checked for variable presence, sample size, variance structure (flagging zero-variance and potential zero-inflation), and factor level sufficiency before any analysis proceeded.

Descriptive statistics and data exploration - We computed descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, coefficient of variation) for each response variable, stratified by location. These summaries characterised baseline patterns, highlighted data dispersion, and informed subsequent model diagnostics and hypothesis testing.
Multivariate community analysis - We used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on a centered and scaled matrix of all response variables to visualize multivariate community structure across locations. We plotted the first two principal components, overlaying 95% confidence ellipses for location groups with at least three samples. To test for significant multivariate differences among locations, we performed Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) based on Euclidean distances with 999 permutations, followed by BH-adjusted pairwise PERMANOVA tests.
Non-parametric tests for location Effects - To provide distribution-free inferences robust to non-normality and heteroscedasticity, we performed Kruskal-Wallis tests for each response variable to assess overall differences among locations. For significant results (p < 0.05), we followed with post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon tests, using Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) False Discovery Rate adjustment for multiple comparisons. Exact p-value computation was disabled in the presence of ties to avoid numerical instabilities. Epsilon-squared was reported as a measure of effect size
Linear and mixed-effects modeling - We modeled the relationship between environmental drivers and each ecological response using both multiple linear regression (LM) and linear mixed-effects models (LMM). The LMMs included a random intercept for `Location` to account for the non-independence of transects within sites, fitted using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) and the BOBYQA optimiser.
Model configuration: To avoid redundancy, if a random intercept for `Location` `(1 | Location)` was specified, `Location` was automatically removed from the model's fixed effects.
Predictor scaling: All continuous predictors were centered and scaled (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) prior to modeling to stabilize estimation and improve convergence. 
Collinearity screening of predictors - To ensure model stability and interpretability, we performed a two-stage screening of fixed-effect predictors to address multicollinearity. First, we calculated pairwise Pearson correlations among all numeric predictors; if any pair exceeded |r| > 0.75, the variable with the higher mean absolute correlation was iteratively removed. Second, we computed Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) using the `car` package. For multi-degree-of-freedom factors, the Generalized VIF (GVIF) was converted to a comparable scale using GVIF^(1/(2*Df)). Predictors with a resultant value > 5 were flagged for potential collinearity and removed. This conservative procedure was performed using a proxy linear model and applied consistently to both Linear Models (LM) and Linear Mixed Models (LMM) to define a robust set of fixed effects.

Hypothesis Testing: For both LM and LMM, we report Type III Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) tables. For LMMs, we preferentially used the Kenward-Roger method for denominator degrees of freedom due to its improved control of Type I error in small, unbalanced samples, with an automatic fallback to the Satterthwaite approximation if the former failed.
Robust Inference: For LMs, we supplement standard errors with Huber-White robust standard errors (HC3) to guard against heteroscedasticity.
Model selection, comparison, and diagnostics - We compared the performance of LM and LMM specifications for each response using Akaike (AIC) and Bayesian (BIC) Information Criteria. For a fair comparison, LMMs were refitted with Maximum Likelihood (ML) before AIC/BIC calculation. A difference in AIC (ΔAIC) greater than 2 was considered evidence that the lower-AIC model provided a meaningfully better fit. We also checked LMMs for singular fits (random-effect variance ≈ 0) using `lme4::isSingular`; a singular fit or a ΔAIC favoring the LM led to the selection of the simpler, more parsimonious LM.
Model assumptions were rigorously validated. For LMs, we inspected residual-versus-fitted, Q-Q, and scale-location plots, and supplemented visual checks with Shapiro-Wilk normality and Breusch-Pagan (NCV) heteroscedasticity tests. For LMMs, we used simulation-based residual diagnostics from the `DHARMa` package. We report adjusted R² for LMs and both marginal (fixed effects) and conditional (fixed + random effects) R² for LMMs.
Univariate Screening of Excluded Predictors - To ensure that predictors dropped during the collinearity screening were not influential in isolation, we ran univariate LMs and LMMs for each excluded predictor against every response variable. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the BH procedure.
Analysis configurations and reproducibility - Analyses were run under the three complementary configurations detailed above. All configurations shared the same validation, modeling, and diagnostic workflow. To ensure full reproducibility, we exported model summaries, Type III ANOVA tables, diagnostic plots, and key session information (R and package versions) for each analysis.
